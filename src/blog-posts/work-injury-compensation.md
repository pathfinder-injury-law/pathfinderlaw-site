*Nothing in this article should be taken as legal advice. Every case is different.*

There is no average payout for a work injury in Virginia, and anyone who quotes you one is guessing. What Virginia workers’ compensation pays is set by law, and it comes in three parts: wage checks while you can’t work, medical treatment, and a set number of weeks of pay if you are left with a permanent loss of use of certain body parts. It does not pay for pain and suffering.

How much you end up with depends on your wages before the accident, how long you are out of work, what treatment you need, whether there is permanent damage, and, before any of that, whether the accident qualifies at all.

## What Virginia Workers’ Comp Pays

| Benefit | What it pays | Law |
|---|---|---|
| Wage checks while you can’t work (temporary total disability) | Two-thirds of your average weekly wage, up to a weekly maximum. For injuries on or after July 1, 2026, the maximum is $1,507.01 a week. | [Va. Code § 65.2-500](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title65.2/chapter5/section65.2-500/) |
| Wage checks on light duty at lower pay (temporary partial disability) | Two-thirds of the difference between what you earned before and what you earn now | [Va. Code § 65.2-502](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title65.2/chapter5/section65.2-502/) |
| Medical treatment | Treatment that is reasonable, necessary, and causally related to the accident | [Va. Code § 65.2-603](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title65.2/chapter6/section65.2-603/) |
| Permanent partial disability | A set number of weeks of pay for permanent loss of use of certain body parts | [Va. Code § 65.2-503](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title65.2/chapter5/section65.2-503/) |

Total compensation is generally capped at 500 weeks, with exceptions such as permanent total incapacity ([Va. Code § 65.2-518](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title65.2/chapter5/section65.2-518/)). Here’s [how long each workers’ comp benefit lasts in Virginia](/how-long-does-workers-comp-last-in-virginia/). Wage checks also have a seven-day waiting period. Here’s [when Virginia workers’ comp starts paying](/virginia-workers-comp-lawyer/when-does-virginia-workers-comp-start-paying/) and [how benefits are calculated](/virginia-workers-comp-lawyer/how-is-workers-comp-calculated-in-virginia/).

## Does It Matter What Body Part Was Injured?

For wage checks and medical care, no. For permanent damage, yes. Virginia law assigns a set number of weeks of pay to the loss of certain body parts ([Va. Code § 65.2-503](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title65.2/chapter5/section65.2-503/)):

| Body part | Weeks of pay for total loss or loss of use |
|---|---|
| Arm | 200 |
| Leg | 175 |
| Hand | 150 |
| Foot | 125 |
| Vision of an eye | 100 |
| Thumb | 60 |
| Hearing of an ear | 50 |
| Index finger | 35 |
| Great toe | 30 |

Most injured workers don’t lose a body part entirely. They are left with a partial loss of use, which a doctor rates as a percentage once they reach [maximum medical improvement](/virginia-workers-comp-lawyer/what-is-maximum-medical-improvement-in-virginia-workers-comp-claims/). For a partial loss, compensation may be awarded in proportion to the rating. For example, a 20 percent loss of use of a leg works out to 20 percent of 175 weeks, or 35 weeks of pay at your compensation rate.

That is why the impairment rating matters so much, and why insurance companies often send injured workers to their own doctors for a second rating. Here’s [what happens after an IME in Virginia](/virginia-workers-comp-lawyer/what-happens-after-a-workers-comp-ime-in-virginia/).

The back and neck are not on the schedule. A spine injury can still lead to permanent partial disability benefits if it causes a permanent loss of use in an arm or leg, or leaves severe scarring.

### Permanent Total Disability

The law treats some injuries as permanent and total incapacity ([Va. Code § 65.2-503](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title65.2/chapter5/section65.2-503/)):

- Loss of both hands, both arms, both feet, both legs, both eyes, or any two of them
- Injury resulting in total paralysis for all practical purposes, as the Commission decides from the medical evidence
- A brain injury so severe that the worker is permanently unemployable in gainful employment

These cases are rare, and they pay for life.

## Why the Same Injury Can Be Worth Very Different Amounts

Two workers with the same surgery can end up with very different results. The difference usually comes from:

- **Whether the accident qualifies.** The accident must arise out of and in the course of your job. A worker can be paralyzed in an accident at work and still get nothing if the accident doesn’t meet Virginia’s legal test. Here’s [how to qualify for workers’ comp in Virginia](/how-to-qualify-for-workers-comp-in-virginia/).
- **Average weekly wage.** Overtime, second jobs, and how long you worked there all affect it, and mistakes here shrink every check.
- **Time out of work and light duty.** A light-duty offer you can’t actually do, or a job search that doesn’t meet the rules, can cut off wage checks.
- **Surgery.** A doctor’s surgical recommendation is often the single biggest driver of a claim’s value, which is why insurance companies fight surgery requests.
- **The impairment rating.** As shown above, a few percentage points can mean weeks of pay.
- **The insurance company’s defenses.** Insurers raise many defenses; a few common ones are that the injury was degenerative, a safety rule was willfully broken, or treatment isn’t related to the accident.

And what you hear from friends and family doesn’t transfer. A settlement in another state, or for a different injury, tells you nothing about a Virginia claim.

## What About a Lump-Sum Settlement?

A settlement is optional. Nobody can force you to settle, and nobody can force the insurance company to offer one. The Commission must approve every settlement ([Va. Code § 65.2-701](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title65.2/chapter7/section65.2-701/)).

Most Virginia cases settle with a **full and final settlement**: one lump sum that closes the entire case, top to bottom, including future medical care for the injury. Settling your case closes everything and ends benefits. Settlements that leave medical benefits open are exceedingly rare. So the lump sum has to account for every future doctor visit, prescription, and surgery related to the injury, because after it is approved you cannot come back for more.

The Commission’s website has benefit calculators, but none of them is a settlement calculator. Settlement value comes from all of the factors above at once.

### Settlements When Medicare Is Involved

Not every settlement needs a **Workers’ Compensation Medicare Set-Aside**. The question comes up when you are already on Medicare, or have a reasonable expectation of getting on Medicare within 30 months, for example because you have applied for Social Security Disability or are 62 and a half. Medicare will review a proposed set-aside only when the settlement is over certain amounts: $25,000 if you are on Medicare, or $250,000 if you expect to be. Medicare says those amounts reflect its own workload, not a safe zone, and that its interests have to be considered in every settlement ([CMS WCMSA Reference Guide](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/coordination-benefits-recovery/workers-comp-set-aside-arrangements)).

When there is a set-aside, part of the settlement goes into a separate, interest-bearing account that can be used only for Medicare-covered treatment of the work injury. The insurance company funds it as part of the settlement. A professional administrator can manage it, but often you are the one who has to: paying the bills from it, keeping records, and sending Medicare a yearly accounting. Medicare won’t pay for treatment related to the work injury until that money is used up, and used properly. Spend it on anything else, and Medicare can refuse to pay for work-injury care until the full amount is accounted for. ## Where Settlement Talks Happen

Many cases settle through direct negotiation. Others settle at a free mediation held through the Commission, where a mediator helps both sides try to reach a lump sum. For a full and final settlement mediation, the Commission requires every party to have a lawyer. If the case doesn’t settle, the dispute goes to a hearing before a deputy commissioner. The insurance company has handled thousands of these. Most injured workers have never been through one.
